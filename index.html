Power the classroom.Not the emptiness.

EcoSwitch is a low-cost, camera-free classroom energy management system that combines PIR motion sensing with 24 GHz mmWave presence detection to control electrical loads zone by zone.

A serious, low-cost, experimental smart-classroom controller.

Lights and fans in classrooms, labs and offices are often left running in empty rooms. PIR sensors are cheap but respond to movement, so they can switch off on someone reading or writing an exam. Cameras avoid that but cost more and raise privacy concerns.

EcoSwitch is a low-cost, offline controller. An ESP32-S3 reads a PIR sensor and a 24 GHz mmWave presence sensor in each of three zones, and turns a zone's loads off only after both sensors have reported that zone clear for a set time. The team is building a three-zone, low-voltage (5 V DC) prototype with an LED light indicator and a DC motor/fan per zone, then testing hybrid against PIR-only and mmWave-only sensing on false-vacancy rate, false-occupancy time, response time and energy saved.

Empty rooms keep drawing power.

Buildings use a large share of electricity. Schools run on fixed timetables with gaps (between lectures, breaks, after practicals, early and late hours) where lights and fans stay on because switching off is nobody's explicit job Hypothesis: to be validated by audit

Surveys of occupancy sensing (Nguyen & Aiello 2013; Dong et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2018) cover cost, accuracy, privacy and installation trade-offs. A lighting-control meta-analysis (Williams et al. 2012) found roughly a quarter average lighting savings, mostly in commercial buildings abroad, so it cannot be applied directly to an Indian campus.

Problem: manual or timer switching wastes energy. Motion-only automation can make false "vacant" calls on still occupants, which annoys users until they bypass or remove it. A low-cost, privacy-preserving approach that stays reliable during low-motion periods is needed.

Existing solutions

PIR switchesWidely sold wall and ceiling occupancy switches.
Dual-technologyPIR plus ultrasonic or microphonic sensing.
BMSScheduling, CO₂ or camera-based occupancy.
ECBCIndia's building code has lighting-control provisions; the clauses for educational buildings still need to be read.

Why they are not more common Hypotheses to test with the college electrician

  • Tariffs and budgets make payback unattractive unless waste is high.
  • Retrofits need wiring changes and trained labour.
  • PIR-only systems can switch off on seated people, so users bypass them.
  • Split incentive: whoever pays the bill is not who chooses or uses the equipment.
  • Cameras raise privacy concerns and need more processing.

Cameras and thermal cameras lose on privacy, cost and processing. CO₂ is too slow. Wi-Fi/BLE misses people without devices. Beam-break needs alignment and counting. PIR and mmWave balance cost, privacy and capability, and their weaknesses differ, which suggests combining them.

The hybrid approach

A PIR sensor reacts to infrared change from moving warm bodies. A 24 GHz mmWave presence/motion sensor reports reflected-energy changes; exact range and detection performance depend on module configuration, placement and calibration Confirm against the S3KM1110 datasheet

Turn ONAs soon as either sensor sees presence in a zone.
Stay ONWhile either sensor still reports presence in that zone.
Turn OFFOnly after both sensors report none, continuously, for the timeout. This is designed to reduce false-vacancy events compared with PIR-only sensing.

What can go wrong with mmWave

None of these is a reason to avoid the sensor; each is a test case in the experimental matrix.

ReflectionsRadar energy bounces off walls and metal, creating returns away from the intended area.
FurnitureMetal cupboards and benches can reflect or shadow part of the zone.
FansA rotating fan is a moving target and can look like occupancy if it is not tuned out.
CurtainsMoving fabric near a window can create motion returns.
RangeDetection range is configurable and limited; each zone must be sized to the module.
PlacementMounting height and angle change what the sensor sees.
CalibrationSensitivity must be tuned per zone and verified in testing.

An empty zone does not force the entire classroom to switch off.

The classroom is split into three independent zones. Each zone has its own mmWave and PIR pair, its own timer, its own light indicator and fan, and its own manual override. The ESP32-S3 evaluates them separately.

Power electronics: switching a 5 V motor with a MOSFET

Low-voltage prototype · 5 V DCOne channel shown; the same circuit is repeated for each zone

Connections to build

  1. Motor positive to the 5 V supply.
  2. Motor negative to the MOSFET drain.
  3. MOSFET source to GND.
  4. 10 kΩ resistor from the gate to GND (gate pulldown).
  5. Flyback diode across the motor terminals, cathode to +5 V and anode to the drain.
  6. Controller GND and load supply GND joined: a common ground.
  7. ESP32-S3 GPIO connects to the MOSFET gate only.
Never direct. A GPIO pin cannot supply motor current. It only drives the gate; the MOSFET carries the load current from the supply.

Part selection: choose a MOSFET whose on-resistance is specified at a 3.3 V gate voltage and whose current rating comfortably exceeds the motor's start-up current. Size the 5 V supply for all three motors starting together.

Known engineering challenges

Fans and curtainsThey reflect radar. Tune sensitivity, limit range, mount away from the fan's line of sight, and test "empty room, fan running".
PlacementHeight and angle change what both sensors see, so several placements are tested.
Sunlight and heatThey can trigger PIR; the test is documented, not assumed.
Zone boundaries and corridorsDetection leaking from a corridor or neighbouring zone causes false occupancy; limit range and tune per zone.

    The research question.

    Core question

    Can a low-cost, camera-free hybrid PIR + 24 GHz mmWave system improve zone-level occupancy detection and reduce unnecessary classroom energy consumption?

    Where the novelty is

    Implementationa complete, low-cost build
    Zone-based controlthree independent zones
    Hybrid sensingPIR with 24 GHz mmWave
    Experimental evaluationagainst PIR-only and mmWave-only
    Classroom-specific testingfans, curtains, corridors
    Not claimed as novel: PIR sensing, mmWave presence sensing, and occupancy-based automation are all established. The contribution is how they are combined, applied per zone and evaluated in a classroom-like setting.

    Literature and research gap

    OverviewsNguyen & Aiello (2013), Dong et al. (2019), Chen et al. (2018): sensor range, accuracy limits, privacy and cost trade-offs.
    In practiceAgarwal et al. (2010): PIR and door sensors drove building control in a university, so campus-scale control is feasible.
    PIR limits and fusionLabeodan et al. (2015) show PIR struggles with low motion; Dodier et al. (2006) show fusing sensors improves detection.
    Savings potentialWilliams et al. (2012): meaningful occupancy-sensor savings, US-focused, hence scenarios plus a local audit.
    RadarManufacturer and chip-vendor documentation describe mmWave presence sensing. Add 2–3 peer-reviewed radar-occupancy papers.
    Gap: a low-cost, camera-free, offline PIR + 24 GHz mmWave controller with zone-level switching, evaluated in an Indian classroom-like setting against PIR-only and mmWave-only baselines, with ceiling-fan interference in focus.

      Safety, privacy and ethics.

      Low-voltage prototype

      The student prototype runs from a low-voltage supply (5 V DC loads). It does not switch live 230 V mains.

      Any building-mains implementation requires appropriately rated hardware, protection and qualified electrical supervision. Students do not wire, open or test anything connected to building mains.

      Risk register

      Limitations: one test room will not generalise to all rooms; no AC control; costs and tariffs are estimates; all sensor performance statements depend on module configuration, placement and calibration.

      Future improvements
